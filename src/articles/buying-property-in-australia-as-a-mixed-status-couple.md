Can one partner be a citizen and the other a foreign buyer? Here's how approvals and surcharges apply when couples co-own property in Australia.

Last checked: 18 November 2026

When one partner is an Australian citizen or permanent resident and the other is considered a foreign person, it can be confusing to understand how property rules apply. There are two separate questions: whether you need FIRB approval, and whether state surcharges apply.

Everyone's situation is different, so this is general information, not advice.

## Who Counts as a "Foreign Person"?

The ATO defines a foreign person as anyone who is not an Australian citizen, not a permanent resident, and not a New Zealand citizen with a special category visa. Some companies and trusts where foreign persons hold substantial interests also count.

## Do You Need FIRB Approval If You Buy Together?

Not if you buy the right way. If the foreign partner buys as **joint tenants** with their spouse who is an Australian citizen, an Australian permanent resident, or a New Zealand citizen eligible for a special category visa, the purchase is exempt from FIRB approval. Because it's exempt, the current ban on foreign persons buying established homes doesn't apply either.

The details matter:

- It has to be joint tenants. Buying as tenants in common doesn't qualify.
- Your partner's citizenship or PR has to already be granted.
- De facto partners can count if the relationship meets the legal definition.
- Tell your conveyancer before the contract is drawn up, so the title isn't set up the wrong way.
- Putting your partner in as a guarantor instead of an owner doesn't create the exemption.

This exemption is about FIRB only. It doesn't switch off state surcharges.

## Joint Ownership: How Surcharges Apply

Each state and territory sets its own foreign buyer surcharges. When a property is purchased jointly, they're generally applied only to the foreign person's share. Two examples:

- In **Victoria** (example), if one partner is a foreign purchaser, the Foreign Purchaser Additional Duty (FPAD) of 8% applies to that partner's interest only. Victoria also has an exemption from FPAD when a foreign purchaser buys a home jointly with a spouse or domestic partner who is a citizen or permanent resident, as long as they live in it as their main residence for 12 months. Check the conditions with the State Revenue Office.
- In **New South Wales** (example), the surcharge purchaser duty (9%) and surcharge land tax (5% each year) apply to the foreign owner's share only. If the foreign partner holds a partner visa (subclass 309 or 820) and has been in Australia for at least 200 days in the 12 months before the contract, they can be treated like a permanent resident for surcharge duty.

Other states differ. Tasmania, for example, also has an exemption for a couple buying a home to live in where only one partner is foreign. For every state and territory, see the [state-by-state table](/articles/buying-property-without-australian-pr-or-citizenship/#state-by-state) in our main visa article.

## Trusts and Companies: Important Trap

If property is purchased through a trust or company, the foreign person tests apply differently. A trust where a foreign person has a substantial interest may be treated as a foreign trust even if other beneficiaries are citizens or permanent residents. In NSW, a discretionary trust can pay surcharge duty if any potential beneficiary is a foreign person. Professional advice is essential for these structures.

## Key Takeaways

- Buying as joint tenants with a citizen or PR spouse means no FIRB approval, even for an established home. Tenants in common doesn't qualify.
- Only the foreign person's share attracts state surcharges.
- Being married doesn't on its own exempt the foreign partner from state surcharges. Some states have couple exemptions, and in NSW, for example, a 309/820 partner visa holder who meets the 200-day test can be exempt.
- Trusts and companies require extra caution. Professional advice is essential.

If you and your partner are planning a purchase, speak with your state revenue office and check the ATO foreign investment pages before signing a contract. Tax, duty and FIRB questions can get technical, so you may also need a lawyer or accountant.
